Where to Start with Process Automation

A simple way to find the automation that will pay off, and the traps that turn promising projects into expensive shelfware.

Adaptum AI22 August 20265 min read

Automation has a reputation problem. For every process that's been quietly running itself for years, there's an ambitious project that consumed a budget and now sits unused. The difference is rarely the technology. It's the choice of what to automate, and how.

A practical way to make that choice well follows.

Find the "boring and frequent" tasks

The best automation candidates are almost always unglamorous: copying data between two systems, chasing the same approvals, generating the same report every Monday, re-keying an order from an email into a spreadsheet. They're easy to overlook precisely because everyone is used to doing them.

A quick test: for a week, ask your team to note any task that made them think "a computer should be doing this." The list you get back is your shortlist.

Score each candidate on three axes

Not every boring task is worth automating. Weigh each one against:

  • Frequency. How often does it happen? Daily beats quarterly.
  • Volume of exceptions. Does it follow the same steps almost every time, or is every case a little different? Consistent processes automate cleanly; messy ones fight back.
  • Cost of a mistake. If the automation gets it wrong, is that a quick fix or a serious problem? Start where errors are visible and recoverable.

The sweet spot is high frequency, low exceptions, and low cost of error. That's where automation pays back quickly and quietly.

Map the process before you automate it

Automating a broken process just makes it break faster. Before building anything, write the steps down exactly as they happen today, including the workarounds nobody documented. Two useful things almost always fall out of this exercise: steps that can simply be removed, and the real rules that govern the "it depends" moments.

Often, cleaning up the process is where half the benefit comes from, before a single line of automation is written.

Keep a human at the decision points

Good automation doesn't remove people; it removes drudgery. Let the system do the fetching, formatting, and moving. Keep a person on the judgement calls, at least until the process has proven itself. A well-placed "review and approve" step is the difference between automation you trust and automation you switch off after the first surprise.

Measure the time you get back

The point of automating a process is to give people their time back for work that actually needs a human. So measure that: hours saved per week, errors avoided, turnaround time reduced. If you can't see the benefit in a number, it's worth asking whether you automated the right thing.

The honest version of "start small"

You don't need a platform, a rules engine, or a transformation programme to begin. You need one repetitive, well-understood task, automated end-to-end, running reliably enough that the team stops thinking about it. Get one of those working, and the next ten become much easier to justify.

That first one is usually closer than it looks. Often it's the task your team complained about this week.
